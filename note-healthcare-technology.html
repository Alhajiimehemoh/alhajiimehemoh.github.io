How technology is transforming healthcare in Sierra Leone

Digital data collection, electronic reporting and better tracking are changing how facilities work. What improves, what holds us back and what helps.

Alhaji Ibrahim Mehemoh

In short

  • Digital tools reduce errors and speed up reporting, but only when the process around them is clear.
  • Internet, power, devices and skills are still the main barriers, and all four need planning.
  • Start with simple tools staff can use and support, then build up.

Healthcare delivery in Sierra Leone is gradually moving from paper-only processes to a mix of paper and digital systems. Facilities and programmes now track performance with Excel sheets, mobile forms, digital logbooks and shared dashboards. Programmes such as nutrition supplement distribution, community health worker activity and ultrasound reporting become much easier to manage once the data is organised.

From paper to digital

Paper registers still matter. They are often the original record and the backup when phones fail. Digital tools work best when they sit alongside paper, not when they pretend paper no longer exists.

What technology improves

  • Fewer copying errors. Numbers entered once on a phone do not need to be re-typed three times.
  • Faster reporting. Data can reach the district in days instead of weeks.
  • Better tracking of supplies. Stock levels and expiry dates are visible before items run out.
  • Clearer decisions. Managers can see which facilities need support this month, not last quarter.

What still holds us back

BarrierWhat helps
Weak or costly internetTools that work offline and sync later; agreed sync days
Unreliable powerPower banks, solar chargers, charging routines
Too few devicesShared devices with clear sign-out, durable cases
Limited IT and data skillsShort practical trainings and follow-up support
Unclear processesAgreed definitions, deadlines and responsibilities
Watch out

Buying devices without training and support is the most common way digital projects stall. Budget for people, not just equipment.

What works in practice

  1. Start with one clear problem, such as late monthly reports.
  2. Choose the simplest tool that solves it and that staff can support.
  3. Train with real, anonymised examples, then follow up within a month.
  4. Share results back with the people who collect the data.
  5. Improve step by step, rather than replacing everything at once.

Protecting patients

More digital data means more responsibility. Collect only what is needed, limit who can see personal information, keep devices locked and never share patient details in group chats.
